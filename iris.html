Iris

Iris (bearded iris, Iris germanica group)

perenniallate spring to early summer

Elegant perennial with sword-like foliage and intricate flowers featuring three upright petals and three drooping falls in a wide range of colors and patterns. Bearded iris varieties bloom in late spring to early summer, while Siberian iris offer more delicate flowers and grass-like foliage. The architectural form provides structure to the garden even when not in bloom, and the rhizomes multiply to create impressive colonies over time.

Iris are elegant perennial flowers characterized by their distinctive three-petaled structure and sword-like foliage, with the most common varieties being bearded iris that bloom in late spring and Siberian iris that offer more delicate blooms. These architectural plants provide year-round garden structure through their striking foliage and multiply via underground rhizomes to form impressive colonies over time.

Care

Summarized from the sources listed below (web research, 2026-11-02). Where sources disagree, the page says so.

What helps it thrive

Full sun (6-8 hours or more) and soil that drains well. Too much moisture rots the rhizomes. Plant with the top of the rhizome at or just above the soil line, since planting too deep is a common mistake. Mulch over the rhizomes can hold moisture and cause rot.

Growing season

Blooms late spring to early summer, then goes dormant shortly after flowering. Remove the flower stalk after bloom so energy does not go into seed. Rebloomers need more attention.

Fall tasks

After a hard frost, cut foliage back hard and remove any spotted or yellowed leaves; bin the debris. In cold climates, protect the first winter: cover the rhizomes with an inch or two of sand topped with a light layer of evergreen boughs after the ground freezes.

Propagation

Division of rhizomes is the main method.

Dividing

Every 3-5 years when blooming declines or the clump crowds (every 2-3 years for rebloomers). Best about 6-8 weeks after flowering, July through September, and no later than 6 weeks before first frost. Lift the clump with a spade or fork and wash off the soil. Keep the young outer rhizomes, each with a firm rhizome and a fan of healthy leaves, and discard the old center and any rhizome with borer damage or rot. Trim leaves to 4-6 inches (about a third of their height), replant 12-18 inches apart in groups of three, water in, and keep moist until new growth appears.

Researched 2026-05-12.

Research (May 12, 2026)
The iris genus encompasses over 300 species of flowering plants, distinguished by their unique flower structure consisting of three upright petals called standards and three drooping petals known as falls. This distinctive form has made iris flowers symbolic across cultures, notably inspiring the fleur-de-lis emblem of French royalty. The most popular garden varieties include bearded iris, which feature fuzzy caterpillar-like structures on their falls and bloom in an extraordinary range of colors from deep purples to pale yellows, often with intricate patterns and color combinations. Siberian iris offer a more refined appearance with grass-like foliage and smaller, more delicate flowers that typically bloom slightly later in the season. Beyond their ornamental value, iris plants provide excellent garden structure through their sword-shaped leaves that remain attractive throughout the growing season. The underground rhizome system allows these perennials to spread and naturalize, creating impressive displays that become more spectacular with age. Many iris varieties are also valued for their fragrance, with some species historically used in perfumery, and their adaptability to various soil conditions makes them reliable performers in diverse garden settings.
