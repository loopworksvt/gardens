Feverfew

Tanacetum parthenium

perennialJune to September

An aromatic herb with small white daisy-like flowers and feathery, deeply divided leaves that release a strong medicinal scent when crushed. This hardy perennial blooms profusely from summer through fall and readily self-seeds in the garden. Historically used for medicinal purposes and valued today for its pest-repelling properties and cottage garden charm.

Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium) is a hardy herbaceous perennial prized for its small white daisy-like flowers, aromatic feathery foliage, and historical medicinal use, particularly for migraine prevention. It is also valued in gardens as a natural pest deterrent and cheerful cottage garden plant that self-seeds prolifically.

What helps it thrive

Full sun in average, medium-moisture, well-drained, humusy soil (Missouri Botanical Garden); full sun to part shade (NC State). It will not tolerate heavy, wet soil (RHS). Plants are short-lived but usually persist by self-seeding; NC State warns it can turn aggressive in ideal conditions and advises deadheading promptly to limit seedlings.

Growing season

Blooms roughly June to September. Short-lived perennial that renews itself by self-sowing.

Research (May 12, 2026)
Feverfew is a robust herbaceous perennial that typically grows 12-24 inches tall, producing masses of small daisy-like flowers from midsummer through fall. The plant's distinctive feathery foliage releases a strong, somewhat bitter scent when crushed, which serves as a natural deterrent to many garden pests including aphids, moths, and mice. Native to southeastern Europe and Asia Minor, feverfew has naturalized throughout much of Europe and North America, thriving in well-drained soils and full sun to partial shade conditions. Historically, feverfew has been one of the most important medicinal herbs in Western herbalism, earning the nickname 'medieval aspirin' for its traditional use in treating headaches, fever, and inflammation. Modern scientific research has focused on the plant's active compounds, particularly parthenolide, which has been studied for its potential anti-inflammatory and antimigraine properties. While some clinical studies suggest modest benefits for migraine prevention, the evidence remains mixed and more research is needed to establish definitive therapeutic effects. In contemporary gardens, feverfew is valued both for its cottage garden aesthetic and practical benefits as a companion plant that naturally repels harmful insects while attracting beneficial pollinators.

Research (Aug 5, 2026)
Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium) is a robust herbaceous perennial native to southeastern Europe and the Caucasus region, now widely naturalized across temperate climates worldwide. It typically grows 12–24 inches tall, forming bushy clumps of deeply lobed, bright yellow-green leaves that emit a strong, somewhat bitter scent when bruised. From midsummer through fall, plants produce prolific clusters of small white daisy-like flowers with prominent yellow centers, making them attractive to beneficial pollinators such as bees and hoverflies. The plant's self-seeding tendency means it can spread readily through a garden, making placement and deadheading important management considerations.

Medicinally, feverfew has a long history of use in European herbal tradition. Modern clinical research has focused primarily on its active compound parthenolide, found in the leaves, which is thought to inhibit platelet aggregation and reduce inflammation. Several clinical studies and systematic reviews have explored its efficacy in migraine prophylaxis, with some evidence supporting modest reductions in migraine frequency when leaves are consumed regularly, though results across trials remain mixed.

In the garden, feverfew's strong aromatic oils make it a valued companion plant. It is reported to repel aphids, whiteflies, moths, and even rodents when planted near susceptible crops or ornamentals. It thrives in average to poor, well-drained soils and tolerates drought once established, requiring minimal care. Regular deadheading prolongs blooming and controls self-seeding. It can be grown from seed, division, or cuttings, and is well suited to herb gardens, cottage borders, and mixed perennial beds.
