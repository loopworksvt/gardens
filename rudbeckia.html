Rudbeckia

Rudbeckia (research below used R. hirta)

perennialJune into fall

Cheerful native perennial with bright yellow daisy-like flowers featuring prominent dark brown or black centers that give them the common name Black-eyed Susan. These tough, drought-tolerant plants bloom from midsummer through fall, providing essential food for butterflies, bees, and seed-eating birds. The sturdy stems rarely need staking, and the plants readily naturalize in meadow gardens and prairie plantings.

Rudbeckia, commonly known as Black-eyed Susan, is a hardy North American native perennial renowned for its vibrant yellow daisy-like flowers with distinctive dark centers that bloom from midsummer through fall. These drought-tolerant plants are ecological powerhouses, providing crucial nectar for pollinators and seeds for birds while requiring minimal maintenance and readily naturalizing in wildflower meadows and prairie restorations.

Care

Summarized from the sources listed below (web research, 2026-11-02). Where sources disagree, the page says so.

What helps it thrive

Full sun in moist, well-drained soil; partial shade is tolerated but blooming is less reliable. Drought tolerant once established. A short-lived perennial that often behaves as an annual or biennial and reseeds freely.

Growing season

Blooms June into fall; deadheading extends the display. Plant in spring after frost danger passes, or in early fall (Clemson favors early fall for perennial types).

Fall tasks

Opinions differ: the Almanac delays cutting back until spring, while Clemson says dead stems can be removed in fall, winter or early spring. Leaving seed heads standing feeds birds and adds winter interest.

Propagation

Seed is easy, sown in fall or spring. Seed needs light to germinate, so leave it uncovered or barely covered, and spring-sown seed needs about three months of cold stratification at 40F. It also self-seeds readily.

Dividing

Division is the best propagation method (Clemson): every 3-4 years, in early spring or fall, to prevent overcrowding. The Almanac says mid to late spring is the best time to transplant.

Research notes

Researched 2026-05-12.

Research (May 12, 2026)
Rudbeckia encompasses approximately 25 species of flowering plants in the sunflower family Asteraceae, native primarily to North America. The genus was named after Swedish botanists Olof Rudbeck the Elder and Younger by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The most widely recognized species, Rudbeckia hirta (Black-eyed Susan), serves as the state flower of Maryland and exemplifies the genus's characteristic bright yellow petals surrounding prominent dark brown to black central cones. These composite flowers are actually composed of numerous tiny florets, with the outer ray petals being sterile and the fertile disc florets in the center producing the seeds that make these plants valuable wildlife food sources.

From an ecological perspective, Rudbeckia species are keystone plants in native ecosystems, supporting over 40 species of butterflies and moths as host plants while their nectar-rich flowers attract bees, butterflies, and other beneficial insects throughout their extended blooming period. The seeds are particularly important for goldfinches, chickadees, and other songbirds during fall and winter months. These plants demonstrate remarkable adaptability, thriving in various soil conditions from clay to sandy soils and tolerating both drought and occasional flooding.

In cultivated landscapes, Rudbeckia has gained popularity for its low-maintenance characteristics and naturalistic appeal. Modern cultivars range from compact 12-inch varieties suitable for containers to tall 6-foot specimens perfect for back-of-border plantings. Their ability to self-sow and spread through underground rhizomes makes them excellent choices for prairie gardens, meadow restorations, and sustainable landscaping projects that prioritize native plant communities and wildlife habitat creation.
