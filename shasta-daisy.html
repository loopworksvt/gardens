Shasta daisy (Leucanthemum × superbum) is a hardy perennial flower featuring classic white petals surrounding bright yellow centers, blooming continuously from early summer through fall with proper deadheading. These reliable garden staples form robust clumps with sturdy stems ideal for cutting, thriving in full sun and well-drained soil while serving as excellent pollinator plants for cottage gardens and naturalized landscapes.

Care

Summarized from the sources listed below (web research, 2026-11-02). Where sources disagree, the page says so.

What helps it thrive

Full sun to light shade and well-drained soil that is not waterlogged. Wet soil in winter can be fatal. Fairly drought tolerant once established and resistant to deer and rabbits. Deadheading spent blooms encourages more flowers.

Growing season

Hardy zones 5-9 (NC State). Blooms early summer into fall with regular deadheading. Plants are somewhat short-lived, which regular division helps offset.

Fall tasks

Cut stems back after the first hard frost (sources say 2-3 inches or about 6 inches above the ground), or leave dead stems standing as winter protection. A few inches of mulch over the crown insulates against extreme cold. Missouri Botanical Garden also suggests cutting stems to the basal leaves after flowering, which may prolong the plant's life.

Propagation

Division is the main and dependable method. Seed works but gives uneven results, and plants reseed readily.

Dividing

Every 2-3 years (some sources say 3-5) to prevent crowding and keep bloom vigorous. Spring or fall are both given; in fall, do it early enough to finish before the ground freezes. The RHS lists early spring or late summer. Cut plants back to about 6 inches in early fall, dig the whole clump keeping as much root ball as possible, cut or pull it apart with clean sharp tools, replant and water sparingly (overwatering is a risk in wet seasons). RHS figures are for UK conditions.

Research notes

Researched 2026-05-12.

Research (May 12, 2026)
Shasta daisies are hybrid perennials created by Luther Burbank in the late 1800s through crossing several Leucanthemum species, resulting in larger, more robust flowers than their wild counterparts. These cheerful blooms typically reach 1-3 feet in height and spread 1-2 feet wide, forming dense clumps of dark green, serrated foliage that provides excellent structure in perennial borders. The flowers, measuring 2-4 inches across, attract beneficial insects including bees, butterflies, and other pollinators, making them valuable for ecological gardens.

Successful cultivation requires full sun exposure and well-drained soil, though they tolerate various soil types from sandy to clay. Regular deadheading extends the blooming period significantly, while dividing clumps every 2-3 years in spring or fall maintains plant vigor and prevents overcrowding. These low-maintenance perennials are drought-tolerant once established and deer-resistant, making them ideal for low-maintenance landscapes. Popular cultivars include 'Becky' (heat-tolerant), 'Alaska' (compact), and 'Crazy Daisy' (double flowers), each offering slight variations in size and flower form while maintaining the classic daisy appearance that makes them indispensable in cottage gardens, cutting gardens, and naturalized meadow plantings.
